00 · Abstract

Can a content page's own search and engagement history, up to a cutoff date, tell us which pages are most likely to see a meaningful drop in search clicks next month? Using 79M rows of GSC and GA4 history across 104 clients, this project builds leakage-safe historical features, defines a future-decline label from the data's own distribution rather than an arbitrary threshold, and trains a Random Forest against a transparent momentum baseline on a strict time-aware split. The model roughly doubles the baseline's Average Precision (0.375 vs. 0.188), but its edge shrinks to statistical noise at the strict top of the queue (Precision@50 of 0.18 against a 0.195 base rate). The output is a ranked, reason-coded action queue — REFRESH_REVIEW, MONITOR, PROTECT, INVESTIGATE, RECOVERY_REVIEW — meant to help a human SEO reviewer decide where to spend limited attention, not to predict how any search engine ranks pages.

01 / 09

Problem framing

An editorial or SEO reviewer has limited time and cannot manually check every content page each month. This project supports one decision: which pages does a human review first, and why?

The unit of analysis is a content page at a cutoff date (content_hash_id × cutoff), not a client or a day. The output is a score, a recommended action, and a set of deterministic reason codes drawn from the same features the model saw. The action a human takes is to open the highest-priority pages first and decide whether they need a content refresh, continued monitoring, or protection from unnecessary changes.

The cost of a wrong call is asymmetric and modest either way: a missed decline costs a delayed refresh; a false alarm costs a reviewer a few minutes checking a page that turns out to be fine. That asymmetry is why this is framed as a ranking problem — surface the most promising candidates near the top of a queue — rather than a hard yes/no classifier making unsupervised decisions.

Data and ML help here because the raw signal (impressions, clicks, position, engagement) is high volume and multi-dimensional across hundreds of thousands of pages — more than a person can scan by eye — but the underlying momentum patterns are learnable from a page's own recent history.

What this does not claim

This project does not predict or explain how Google's (or any) search algorithm ranks pages, and it does not claim that refreshing a flagged page will cause its traffic to recover. "Decline" is an operational label defined from observed historical click behavior — not a measure of content quality.

02 / 09

Data safety

The data comes from the gated FlyRank/internship-warehouse release on Hugging Face, queried directly with DuckDB over hf:// Parquet paths rather than downloading the full warehouse. Three tables were used: fact_content_daily_performance (78.8M rows, 2025-01-27 → 2026-06-30), dim_clients, and dim_content (104 clients, 519,606 content pages).

KeptExcluded / never used as a feature
GSC impressions, clicks, avg. position; GA4 pageviews, engaged sessions, engagement seconds, scroll events — all aggregated over recent_28 / prior_28 windows before a cutoff future_clicks, future_impressions, future_organic_sessions — used only to build the label, never as a model feature
content_hash_id / client_hash_id — pseudonymous, used for grouping and joins only Any raw client- or page-identifying field (URL, client name) — the warehouse release is pseudonymized at source and nothing client-identifying was reintroduced in work/

Leakage risks considered

  • Label-derived fields. The only columns computed from the post-cutoff window are future_*, and they feed exclusively into opportunity_label — never into FEATURE_COLS.
  • Repeated pages across cutoffs. The same page can appear at more than one cutoff date. Because each cutoff's feature window and label window are strictly time-separated, this is treated as a rolling-origin design rather than leakage — the same convention used for time-series cross-validation.
  • Threshold and split fitting. The decline threshold (25th percentile of future_click_ratio) and all preprocessing are computed on the training split only, never on test.
  • Exact-duplicate page-days. A grain probe on one mid-panel month found no duplicate (content_hash_id, report_date) rows; the pipeline still dedupes defensively with ANY_VALUE() upstream of every aggregation.

Nothing client-identifying appears anywhere in this report or in work/.

03 / 09

Baseline

The baseline is a transparent momentum rule with no fitted weights: a page should be reviewed first when its clicks are falling and its search position is getting worse, relative to its own recent history.

baseline score

baseline_score = −click_ratio_recent_prior + position_change

A higher score means more concerning momentum. Because the weights aren't learned, this is a fair, legible comparison point: any ML model claiming an improvement has to beat a rule any reviewer could compute by hand. Evaluated on the identical test rows and labels as the model (see Evaluation), the baseline scores 0.120 Precision@50 and 0.188 Average Precision — the second of which is below the population's 0.195 base rate.

04 / 09

Model / analysis

A Random Forest classifier (300 trees, max depth 6, min 20 samples/leaf, class_weight="balanced") was chosen for this lane because the features are tabular, non-linear interactions between momentum signals are plausible (e.g. falling clicks matter more when position is also worsening), and tree ensembles give an interpretable, sanity-checkable feature-importance ranking — important for a tool whose whole value is that a reviewer can trust why a page was flagged.

Target

One-sentence definition: a page is labeled a positive "opportunity" when its 28-day post-cutoff click ratio (future_click_ratio = (future_clicks + 1) / (recent_clicks + 1)) falls at or below the 25th percentile of that ratio's distribution across eligible training pages — a threshold of 0.667, read from the data rather than assumed in advance.

Feature list

Eligibility requires the client's gsc_data_start to precede the feature window by at least 55 days, gsc_data_available IS TRUE for the days used (avoiding NULL-as-available errors), and recent_impressions at or above a visibility floor of 107 — the median among visible pages — so that a page with negligible search visibility isn't scored as "declining."

Evaluation

The split is time-aware, not random: two cutoffs (2025-09-30, 2025-11-30) form the training set, and a strictly later cutoff (2026-01-31) is held out as test — mirroring how the system would actually run in production, where today's model has to score pages it has never seen the future of.

After the eligibility and feature-completeness filters, training has 48,697 rows (25.6% positive) and test has 30,302 rows (19.5% positive). Because the label is defined as the training set's own 25th percentile, a ~25% training positive rate is expected by design, not a sign of an imbalanced dataset needing correction — this is why the evaluation leans on Precision@K, Recall@K, and Average Precision rather than accuracy.

At random, 50 pages drawn from a 19.5%-positive population would be expected to contain ~9.75 positives. The Random Forest found 9 — essentially chance — while the baseline found 6, below chance. Precision@50 alone does not support a strong claim that the model wins at the very top of the queue.

Reading the errors

At the default 0.5 probability threshold on the test set: 373 false negatives (real declines the model missed) against 10,709 false positives (pages flagged that did not decline). That lopsided count is largely a side effect of class_weight="balanced" pushing probabilities upward against an already-elevated 19.5% base rate — it's a reason to use the ranked score for prioritization, as this project does, rather than treating 0.5 as a hard yes/no cutoff. The ranked queue, not the binary label, is the actual product.

06 / 09

Interpretation

fig. 2 — random forest feature importance

recent_clicks dominates at 38% of total importance, followed by click_volatility_56d (23%) and click_ratio_recent_prior (13%). This is plausible rather than suspicious: pages with more recent click activity simply have more room to register a measurable change in future_click_ratio, and volatility in daily clicks is a genuinely different signal from a smooth ratio change. No single feature approaches a near-total share of importance, which is the sanity check against an accidentally leaked or label-derived column.

In plain words, the model has effectively learned a more nuanced version of "watch pages with meaningful recent traffic whose click pattern is choppy or already trending down" — closer to the baseline's intuition than to anything exotic, which is consistent with the model's real but modest edge over that same baseline.

A well-understood negative result

position_change and engagement_rate_change — the two features closest to the baseline rule's own logic — rank lowest in importance (0.4% and 0.5%). The model found more signal in the shape of click behavior itself than in the position-based logic the baseline relies on almost entirely, which helps explain why the baseline underperforms its own base rate.

Recommendation

Each ranked page carries deterministic reason codes computed from the same recent_* / prior_* features the model used, so a reviewer never has to inspect the model to understand a recommendation.

Reason codeTrigger
DECLINING_CLICKSClick ratio in the bottom quartile
DECLINING_IMPRESSIONSImpression ratio in the bottom quartile
POSITION_WORSENINGPosition change worse than a small noise band
ORGANIC_SESSION_DROPOrganic sessions falling recent vs. prior
ENGAGEMENT_DECLINEEngagement rate falling recent vs. prior
HIGH_VISIBILITY_LOW_CLICKSAbove-median impressions, below-median clicks
RECOVERY_SIGNALClick ratio in the top quartile
STABLE_MONITORNone of the above triggered

Reason codes combine into one of five actions:

REFRESH_REVIEW a decline signal is present —  INVESTIGATE decline and recovery signals conflict —  RECOVERY_REVIEW recovery signal alone —  PROTECT strong visibility with early volatility —  MONITOR everything else near the top of the queue.

A FlyRank editor would work this list top to bottom: open REFRESH_REVIEW pages first, skim MONITOR pages for anything the reason codes missed, and treat RECOVERY_REVIEW as a lower-urgency check-in. Given the Precision@50 result above, confidence in the exact top 50 is moderate, not high — the model's real advantage is in overall ranking quality (Average Precision), so a reviewer should expect the queue to be directionally useful across its first few hundred rows rather than perfectly ordered in its first 50.

Environment: duckdb, pandas, numpy, scikit-learn, matplotlib, huggingface_hub — installed fresh at the top of the notebook, no pinned lockfile beyond that %pip install cell.

This evaluation is a strict time-aware holdout, not a sealed/blind sample in the stronger sense — the 2026-01-31 test cutoff and its label window (through 2026-02-28) were known at analysis time, just never used to fit the model or choose the decline threshold. The cell that builds this split (CUTOFF_SPECS, cell 17) and the metrics file it produces (work/outputs/capstone_model_comparison.json) are both committed, so "trained on train, scored once on test" is checkable from the repo rather than taken on faith.
